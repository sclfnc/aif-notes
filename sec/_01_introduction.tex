\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

% ── slides L1 p.4–12 ──
Artificial intelligence (AI) is a large family of methods: machine learning
(ML) is one member, deep learning (DL) is a branch of ML, and the large language
models (LLMs) behind tools such as ChatGPT and Copilot are a branch of DL. This
course covers ``all AI, but learning'', in the lecturer's words: methods that
answer broader questions than ML and that run, mostly unseen, inside many
AI-based tools. Some learning may still appear; \cref{sec:intro-hybrid}
explains the choice.

% Grouping of the lectures of p.5 into the four blocks of p.4; planning sits
% with logical agents as in AIMA part III (p.6); the slides place multi-agent
% systems in no block.
After a lecture on the design of intelligent agents (\cref{sec:agents}), the
course runs in four blocks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Solving problems by search}: uninformed and informed search,
    local search, constraint satisfaction problems, search in games.
  \item \textbf{Evolutionary optimization and artificial life}: evolutionary
    optimization, novelty-based search and quality-diversity, artificial life.
  \item \textbf{Logical agents}: inference in propositional and first-order
    logic, planning.
  \item \textbf{Probabilistic agents}: Bayesian networks, hidden Markov models.
\end{itemize}
Multi-agent systems close the course.

\subsection{Symbolic AI}\label{sec:intro-symbolic}

% ── slides L1 p.19 ──
Symbolic AI, the dominant paradigm from the 1950s to the 1990s, represents the
world at a high level, in a form that humans can read and interpret. Its
building block is the symbol.

\begin{definition}[Symbol]\label{def:symbol}
  A \textbf{symbol} is a meaningful pattern that can be manipulated.
\end{definition}

A symbol stands for something else, as the string \texttt{cat} stands for the
animal, and a program can compare, copy and combine it into rules. Semiotics,
the study of signs, calls the pattern the \textbf{signifier} and what it stands
for the \textbf{signified}.

Where do the symbols of a machine come from? Either humans provide them, and
then they mean something only through someone who already had symbols: a
chicken-and-egg problem. Or the computer builds its own low-level representations from raw
data, which is sub-symbolic manipulation (\cref{sec:intro-subsymbolic}).

\subsection{The symbol grounding problem}\label{sec:intro-grounding}

% ── slides L1 p.20 ──
With the first answer, the machine manipulates symbols without knowing what
they stand for. Harnad named this difficulty.

\begin{definition}[Symbol grounding problem]\label{def:grounding}
  The \textbf{symbol grounding problem} asks how a symbol is connected to the
  real-world object it stands for: what makes the connection, who makes it,
  and how. In Harnad's words: ``How can the semantic interpretation of a
  formal symbol system be made intrinsic to the system, rather than just
  parasitic on the meanings in our heads?''\footnote{S.~Harnad, ``The symbol
  grounding problem'', \emph{Physica D} 42 (1990), pp.~335--346.}
\end{definition}

A symbol represents something outside the system, so the answer requires
``jumping out of the system'', in Hofstadter's phrase. Formal logic meets the
same limit in Gödel's incompleteness theorem: every consistent, effectively
axiomatized system that can express elementary arithmetic has a true statement
that it cannot prove, and establishing that statement takes a step outside.

\subsection{The physical symbol system hypothesis}\label{sec:intro-pssh}

% ── slides L1 p.21 ──
If symbols can be grounded, is manipulating them enough for intelligence?
Newell and Simon answered yes, as a hypothesis.

\begin{definition}[Physical symbol system]\label{def:pss}
  A \textbf{physical symbol system} is a system that manipulates symbols to
  produce other symbols. It is \emph{physical} because the manipulation is a
  physical process, which consumes energy.
\end{definition}

In the lecture's terms, humans are physical symbol systems, and so are digital
computers, since both manipulate symbols. The \textbf{physical symbol system
hypothesis} (PSSH) states:
\begin{quote}
  A physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general
  intelligent action.\footnote{A.~Newell and H.~A.~Simon, ``Computer science as
  empirical inquiry: symbols and search'', \emph{Communications of the ACM}
  19(3) (1976), pp.~113--126.}
\end{quote}
% The slide drops "general"; the wording and both glosses below follow the
% 1976 paper.
Newell and Simon gloss both halves. \emph{Necessary}: ``any system that
exhibits general intelligence will prove upon analysis to be a physical symbol
system''. \emph{Sufficient}: ``any physical symbol system of sufficient size can
be organized further to exhibit general intelligence''. The claim is empirical,
so it is a hypothesis and not a theorem: evidence can support or refute it. If
it holds, a suitably organized digital computer can act with general
intelligence.

\subsection{Intelligence is not consciousness}\label{sec:intro-consciousness}

% ── slides L1 p.22 ──
Intelligence and consciousness are different properties: a system that acts
intelligently need not be conscious. Consciousness is usually tied to
subjective experience: in Nagel's sense,\footnote{T.~Nagel, ``What is it like
to be a bat?'', \emph{The Philosophical Review} 83(4) (1974).} a being is
conscious if there is something it is like to be that being. Such experience is
hard to detect from outside, even by dissecting a system and studying its
behavior. A \textbf{zombie}, which behaves exactly like a person but has no
experience, would pass every behavioral test. Searle's Chinese room makes a
related point about understanding: a person who follows written rules to
manipulate Chinese characters answers questions in Chinese without
understanding any.\footnote{J.~R.~Searle, ``Minds, brains, and programs'',
\emph{Behavioral and Brain Sciences} 3(3) (1980).}

Where consciousness comes from, and whether a substrate other than neurons
could support it, the lecture leaves open.

\subsection{Sub-symbolic representations}\label{sec:intro-subsymbolic}

% ── slides L1 p.24–25 (images only; the reading follows the slide titles) ──
Symbolic descriptions break down when the world varies more than rules can
anticipate; the alternative lets the machine build its own representation.

\begin{definition}[Sub-symbolic representation]\label{def:subsymbolic}
  A \textbf{sub-symbolic representation} encodes a concept as a numeric pattern
  spread over many low-level units, such as the weights of a neural network,
  instead of as a single symbol that a human can read.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Recognizing a cat]\label{ex:cat}
  This example contrasts the two approaches. A symbolic system describes a cat
  through symbols for its parts, such as two eyes, whiskers and pointed ears,
  and through rules that combine them. Real photographs of cats vary in pose,
  breed, lighting and framing, and each new variation breaks some rule. A
  sub-symbolic system learns its representation from many example images and
  absorbs the variation, but no human can read that representation.
\end{example}

\subsection{Models of the world}\label{sec:intro-models}

% ── slides L1 p.26 (images only: a map of Pisa; house features pointing to a
% selling price; a path through a labyrinth) ──
An AI system reasons about a \textbf{model of the world}: a representation of
the part of reality that it works on. The lecture distinguishes three kinds, by
the question that each answers.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Descriptive}: how is the world? A map of Pisa.
  \item \textbf{Predictive}: what value will an unobserved quantity take? The
    selling price of a house from its floor area, number of rooms and energy
    efficiency; machine learning builds such models.
  \item \textbf{Decisional}: what should be done to reach a goal? The path
    through a labyrinth; the search methods of the course answer such
    questions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hybrid systems}\label{sec:intro-hybrid}

% ── slides L1 p.27–28; p.29–31 (AI scientist, agentic web, evolutionary
% computation) are outside the agreed scope ──
Machine learning maps inputs $X$ to outputs $Y$. Building the map (training)
needs huge amounts of data, more for more complex mappings; using it
(inference) needs expensive memory, time and accelerators such as GPUs and
TPUs. Deep learning needs even more of both. It still works very well in many
applications, and the lecture states the course's position explicitly: ``This course is not saying
`don't use deep learning'. It's saying `don't use deep learning only'.'' A
\textbf{hybrid system} combines a learned model with components that are not
learned.

\begin{example}[Retrieval-augmented generation]\label{ex:rag}
  Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a worked case of a hybrid system. A
  \textbf{retriever} fetches the documents relevant to a question from an
  external memory, such as Wikipedia, and a \textbf{generator}, a language model,
  writes the answer from the question and those documents. We can extend the
  memory to a new domain or update it with the news without retraining, and
  each answer can cite its source document, which makes it easier to
  interpret.
\end{example}
